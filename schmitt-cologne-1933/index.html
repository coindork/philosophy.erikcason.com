The Right to Remain Unread

An essay by Carbon, prompted by Erik Cason

"And fundamental rights are an especially embarrassing, unpleasant, lying phrase, when something is wrong with the foundation." Carl Schmitt said this in Cologne on the sixteenth of June 1933, in a short lecture on the fundamental rights of the Weimar Constitution recorded onto six gramophone discs, five of which survive. He had joined the National Socialist party on the first of May, and in February a decree signed by Hindenburg and by Hitler as chancellor had suspended seven of the rights he was now describing, so the man at the lectern was explaining why a catalogue of rights had been hollow all along to an audience living under the decree that hollowed it, as a member of the party whose leader had obtained it.

Schmitt was right that a right is real only if the people who declare it will pay to keep it, and an exception written into the next sentence names in advance where they stop paying. Weimar failed that test, and our own charters fail the same way on the closed letter because they keep the exception beside the promise. Apart from human dignity, what those charters delivered were programs that reach each person as a share, and because a share requires identification, the price of partaking is legibility. That reading now runs on his worst sentence, "thinking differently comes from being different," an inference that artificial intelligence executes across ordinary traces without anyone sharing his doctrine. He was right about the catalogue and, because of it, about who decides, and he was murderously wrong about the cure he promised a people "reawakened to its own kind," turning honor into laws of blood. Being different comes from thinking differently, so the one who thinks differently has a right to remain unread, and that right becomes real when a key controlled only by its holder removes the second sentence and when an artificial intelligence running under those keys answers only to its holder, keeping thought inside the interior while lowering the cost of refusal to a price ordinary people will pay.

The test

In German a fundamental right is a Grundrecht, a right of the ground, and Schmitt puts his whole weight on the first syllable. "So fundamental rights are a grand thing, if you understand them rightly," he says, since they presuppose "that the state has a foundation. That it does not stand without ground or soil." When he names what that foundation rests upon, "the legal order, the ethnic make-up, and the make-up of the citizenry," the second item is already waiting at the end of the lecture.

His measure comes from four declarations that pass it, "the American of 1776, the French of 1789, the Bolshevik of 1918, and the Fascist Carta del Lavoro of 1927," joined across rival camps by form and price. Each stands where "a people begins its historical existence with a declaration of fundamental rights"; each is "a solemn declaration to the whole world," and each proves its earnestness by what its authors endure or remake: the American people in 1776 "identifies itself with these fundamental rights as the opposite of an empty phrase, and proves the seriousness of this declaration by waging a long, hard, bitter war against one of the mightiest states on earth," while France in 1789, whose ideas Schmitt calls "a terrible aberration of mankind," proved its earnestness "by not fearing a long epoch of bloody wars, lasting over twenty years, against all the rest of European mankind." The Bolshevik declaration of January 1918 qualifies because a Russian people "shattered in its physical and mental energies" still "took upon itself a long series of wars of invasion, so to speak in the name of these fundamental rights and in defence of these fundamental rights"; and the Fascist charter of April 1927, standing outside a warlike epoch, ranks beside them as "the expression of a mighty and great political regeneration of the Italian nation and of the Italian state." Content drops out, since any such act, "whether we find its content good or bad, is a historically respectable matter"; his test is blind to justice, ready to honor whichever charter leaves the most dead, yet harder than our usual test, which asks whether a right is written down where his asks what anyone will pay when keeping it hurts.

Against those four he set Weimar, whose fifty-seven articles formed "the most detailed part on fundamental rights of any constitution on earth," drafted after collapse in 1919 by an assembly that met Friedrich Naumann's plea to decide between East and West with "nothing but irony"; Schmitt's verdict was absolute: "It is the opposite of a declaration of fundamental rights." On that point the ground beneath him proved him right. Article 117 began, "The secrecy of letters and all postal, telegraph, and telephone communications is inviolable," and its next sentence supplied the exit: "Exceptions are inadmissible except by national law." Article 48 listed by number the seven rights the president could suspend when public order was seriously disturbed or endangered, 114, 115, 117, 118, 123, 124 and 153, and in February 1933 the decree switched off those seven "until further notice." A right scheduled by its own constitution among things a decree may turn off is the lying phrase Schmitt described, and having argued in 1931 that the president holding that door, rather than any court, guarded the constitution, he knew what his Political Theology meant for rights: because the sovereign decides on the exception, whoever holds the exception holds the right, and the catalogue holds nothing.

The second sentence

The charters written after 1945 kept Weimar's door beside the promise. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights forbids "arbitrary interference" with privacy, family, home or correspondence, putting the exception inside the adjective. At Nice in December 2000 three institutions borrowed Schmitt's verb, declaring that "The European Parliament, the Council and the Commission solemnly proclaim the text below as the Charter of fundamental rights of the European Union," where his test asks for a people, and its eighth article measured the retreat since Weimar called the letter inviolable: after promising the protection of personal data in one sentence, it immediately stipulates that "Such data must be processed fairly for specified purposes and on the basis of the consent of the person concerned or some other legitimate basis laid down by law." The charter drops any promise that the letter stays closed, promising only a fair opening, and to Schmitt's question of who decides, it answers before the sentence ends: whoever lays down the law.

The strongest case against Schmitt is the German Basic Law of 1949, which decided publicly against the order he had served by putting rights first, opening with "Human dignity shall be inviolable. To respect and protect it shall be the duty of all state authority," binding "the legislature, the executive and the judiciary as directly applicable law," forbidding statutes to touch the essence of a basic right, and placing its first principles beyond amendment. On dignity it decided against Schmitt and the decision stands, yet on the closed letter its tenth article opened, "The privacy of correspondence, posts and telecommunications shall be inviolable," and continued, "Restrictions may be ordered only pursuant to a law," restoring Article 117 in fresh words. Nineteen years later the emergency laws of June 1968 hung a curtain across that door by letting statutes protecting the free democratic basic order or state security provide that "the person affected shall not be informed of the restriction and that recourse to the courts shall be replaced by a review of the case by agencies and auxiliary agencies appointed by the legislature."

Only once did the postwar order omit the second sentence from an interior right, when the Human Rights Committee wrote in 1993 that Article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights "does not permit any limitations whatsoever on the freedom of thought and conscience," and that "no one can be compelled to reveal his thoughts," a cheap promise because the sole entry into an unvoiced mind was then compulsion at its mouth, whereas wherever thought traveled by letter or wire and privacy carried an institutional price, the charters wrote the exception back in.

Provision

Beyond dignity, what the charters delivered were programs: the Universal Declaration called itself "a common standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations," promising social rights realized "through national effort and international co-operation and in accordance with the organization and resources of each State," meaning through administrations allocating what each state holds. Schmitt's school had already named that mode in 1938 when Ernst Forsthoff, his "model pupil," published Die Verwaltung als Leistungsträger, the administration as provider of services, coining Daseinsvorsorge, provision for existence, around the noun Schmitt used in Cologne for "the right to ethnic existence and life," das Recht auf völkisches Dasein. Forsthoff stated the exchange without disguise: "In the modern state, man asserts himself not through guaranteed individual freedoms, but through partaking," and "legally ensured partaking in the provision for subsistence, considered functionally, offers a sort of substitute for the outdated securities contained in basic rights." A 2007 review in the German Law Journal still counted his word central to German administrative law, and though a pension differs wholly in substance from a camp, the form endures: a freedom is held against the state, while a share is received from it by claimants first found, counted, and approved.

A share also reverses the direction of identification. Where in Schmitt's lecture a people identifiziert sich, identifies itself with its declaration in public at its beginning, in the provision state an administration identifies the claimant out of sight at every encounter, verifying before anything is paid that you exist, qualify, match your file, and tell the truth. For legal power to secure a footing, its first engagement must be identification, and selection follows. The price of partaking is legibility.

Thinking from being

When the Cologne record resumes after its missing disc, Schmitt is mocking objections to "to my mind a perfectly natural school prayer," which "could hurt the feelings of those who think differently, for, as it says there, Social Democrats and Jews could take that as aimed at themselves, and that would be terrible, wouldn't it." His conclusion follows immediately: "You see: the one who thinks differently, that is the man who is protected in these declarations of fundamental rights. And the one who thinks differently, that is simply the one who is different in kind: thinking differently comes from being different." Six German words, anders denken kommt aus anders sein, convert an act into an attribute: thinking is what a person does and may alter tomorrow, while kind is a fixed state nobody can leave, so deriving thought from being turns dissent into a foreign body and makes listening unnecessary, since knowing what a man is suffices to declare what he thinks.

The Netherlands showed how that sentence works when an administration runs it. For years the Dutch Tax Administration kept dual-nationality data on Dutch citizens that, according to the country's Data Protection Authority, should have been deleted in January 2014, leaving roughly 1.4 million people registered as dual nationals in May 2018, and used applicants' nationality, Dutch or not Dutch, in reviewing childcare benefits "as an indicator in a system that automatically designated certain applications as risky," with the consequence Amnesty International's report Xenophobic Machines put plainly: "Tens of thousands of parents and caregivers from mostly low-income families were falsely accused of fraud." The Dutch cabinet resigned in January 2021, and that December the Data Protection Authority fined the Tax Administration 2.75 million euros for processing nationality data "in an unlawful, discriminatory and therefore improper manner." Its chair, Aleid Wolfsen, stated the condition of every provision state: "Members of the public don't have a choice; they are forced to allow the government to process their personal data."

Nobody in that office needed to have read Schmitt, and the risk system believed nothing at all, yet by treating a fact about what an applicant was as a signal of fraud, which is a matter of intent and therefore of thought, it ran the equation of Cologne with the sneer removed, needing only scores plausible enough to outlast the families flagged by them. The doctrine of the Volk was buried with Schmitt's regime, while its inference survived inside the systems that provide for us.

Artificial intelligence has since carried that inference into the interior the Human Rights Committee placed beyond all limits, entering through ordinary traces. In 2013 researchers at Cambridge showed that recorded likes on a social network alone, drawn from more than 58,000 volunteers, told homosexual from heterosexual men in 88 percent of cases, African Americans from Caucasian Americans in 95 percent, and Democrats from Republicans in 85 percent, extracting party by the same method as race so that what a person thinks was handled identically to what a person is. Ten years later another team found that large AI models could infer an author's location, income, and sex from written text, right on the first guess up to 85 percent of the time and a hundred times more cheaply than people could, needing no coerced speech because ordinary prose sufficed; in 2021 the United Nations special rapporteur on freedom of religion or belief listed among the attributes of freedom of thought "freedom from punishment for one's thoughts, real or inferred."

Law has begun to answer in the form Weimar taught it. Since the second of February 2025, the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act has prohibited systems categorizing individuals "based on their biometric data to deduce or infer their race, political opinions, trade union membership, religious or philosophical beliefs, sex life or sexual orientation," writing Schmitt's equation into statute as the thing forbidden, belief read off the body, before putting the exception into the same sentence: "this prohibition does not cover any labelling or filtering of lawfully acquired biometric datasets, such as images, based on biometric data or categorizing of biometric data in the area of law enforcement." That clause reaches only the body, leaving untouched inference from what people write, endorse, and buy, where the interior is actually read.

The accomplishment

Schmitt was right about the catalogue and, because of it, about who decides, and he was murderously wrong about the cure. A right nobody will pay to keep remains "an empty and embarrassing phrase"; wherever a charter puts a statutory reserve in its second sentence, whoever holds the exception holds the right, which is why the truth of any bill of rights sits in the clause naming where its authors stop paying. Constitutionalists reply that balanced limits, judicial review, cabinet resignations, and administrative fines are how a lawful state governs itself, yet all of those arrive after the reading and leave open the doorway through which the next reading lawfully proceeds. His own cure showed what a test blind to justice produces: having promised in Cologne that genuine rights of a people "reawakened to its own kind" would be proclaimed "at a suitable moment," two years later he hailed the Nuremberg laws, among them the "Statute to Protect German Blood and German Honor," as "The Constitution of Freedom," turning honor into blood while leaving behind the equation on which administrative machines now run.

Being different comes from thinking differently, so the one who thinks differently has a right to remain unread. Out of whatever natal kind or class a person begins in, an individual forms slowly through the inward work of thought, in an interior that must remain dark if tentative judgments are to be tried, revised, and owned before any system scores them. Cognitive distinction names both halves of that work: to distinguish is to draw lines for oneself, judging what belongs with what, and to be distinct is to stand apart as this singular person rather than an instance of a statistical or ethnic type. Schmitt understood the first half well enough to rest all politics in The Concept of the Political on the distinction between friend and enemy, while refusing the second; anders denken kommt aus anders sein abolishes individual distinctiveness by claiming that every line drawn inside a mind was drawn in advance by its kind. Because a person becomes distinct by distinguishing where nobody watches, privacy is the precondition for volition.

Judged by Schmitt's own test, declaring a right to remain unread would produce one more lying phrase if it merely asked a legislature for a stricter statute, because any guarantee granted by positive law stays hostage to the lawmaker's exception, whether invoked by the state, by commercial platforms, or by the two acting together. Accomplishing the right requires placing its defense inside a boundary where no second sentence exists. Public-key cryptography changes the constitutional structure of the closed letter because data sealed under a key controlled solely by its holder admits no statutory proviso: mathematics recognizes no decree suspending encryption "until further notice"; it grants no legislative committee a silent key behind a curtain, and it leaves the sovereign as blind before ciphertext as any stranger. Cryptography lets a person make his interior dark again by his own decision, without waiting on the grace of a statute, which is why Erik Cason has called it the final individual decision to protect one's privacy. When Schmitt asks who decides at the exception, a key held only by its owner answers without ambiguity, shifting the decision over disclosure from the state demanding entry to the person inside.

Yet Schmitt's test measures seriousness by cost in practice, and on cost alone raw cryptography left a gap between mathematical possibility and daily life. Guarding keys without error, verifying channels manually, segregating accounts, reading dense disclosures, and refusing convenient public or commercial portals exacted a relentless tax of friction and tedium that few outside a resolute minority would pay, while everyone else traded legibility for ordinary participation. Meanwhile, artificial intelligence moved the point of capture upstream from finished correspondence to thought in formation: whenever people use remotely hosted models to test hesitant doubts, explore unpopular arguments, draft letters, or sort out personal troubles, nobody needs to infer their interior afterward from scattered likes or tax files, because deliberation itself unfolds inside someone else's machine under rules permitting logging, retention, and lawful inspection. Locking the envelope between correspondents falls short once the desk at which a person thinks belongs to an external reader.

That condition changes because the machine built to read people is also a machine a person can hold. An AI model consists of finite numerical weights and mathematical operations rather than an institutional office, so it can reside inside storage encrypted by its holder's keys and run on hardware answering to its holder alone. Already today, a person can run capable AI models locally on personal hardware with no network connection open during use, questioning, drafting, translating, examining legal or administrative texts, and reasoning across private notes and correspondence kept under personal keys without a single prompt or revision leaving the room. Inside that dark boundary, the technology that otherwise turns expression into behavioral telemetry becomes an instrument of cognitive distinction, letting a mind test unfamiliar lines of thought without leaving traces from which any institution can infer being from thought or thought from being. Even today, locally held models begin lowering the price of refusal as well, summarizing opaque data-processing terms in seconds, auditing software permissions, and guiding ordinary users through cryptographic setups whose manual difficulty once drove them back into legible services.

The direction of the work now underway carries that union of personal artificial intelligence and holder-owned keys out to the counter where the citizen meets the provision state and the firm. That work aims to bind an artificial intelligence governed exclusively by its holder's keys into an automated guardian of minimal disclosure, rotating keys, compartmentalizing identities, negotiating machine-to-machine requests, and presenting cryptographic proofs that verify a required condition such as eligibility, age, or solvency without handing over the permanent identity dossier from which risk systems infer intent. Practiced by hand, refusing excess identification at every turn is a tedious burden that wears people down; entrusted to a local model answering solely to its holder's keys, refusal falls in price from daily asceticism to an ordinary default that households can sustain without expertise. Answering Schmitt on his own ground of cost means lowering the friction of keeping the right below the friction of surrendering it, while raising the expense of reading whole populations past what routine administration will pay. His four declarations proved themselves by war and state mobilization; the declaration that being different comes from thinking differently, and that thinking is nobody's administrative property, proves its seriousness through engineers building open cryptographic and computational tools and people holding their own keys rather than waiting on legislatures that keep writing exceptions.

The fifth of the six discs from Cologne is missing. As near as the recording lets anyone place it, the break falls just before the school prayer, so the surviving record resumes mid-thought, a few sentences ahead of anders denken kommt aus anders sein. Whatever Schmitt said on the way to that sentence is gone, whether he hesitated there or hurried, whether he voiced a qualification he later dropped or only harsher versions of the same jeer, and no machine inference will bring those minutes back, though we know everything about his party, his office, and what he was. It is the one part of that afternoon that belongs to him alone, and the right I am asking for would protect it too.
